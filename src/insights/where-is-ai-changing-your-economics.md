Artificial intelligence has created no shortage of activity inside organisations.

Pilots are multiplying. Productivity tools are spreading. Teams are experimenting with copilots, agents and automation. Boards are asking for AI strategies, while business units are assembling lists of potential use cases.

Much of this activity is useful. But it starts with the wrong question.

The conventional starting point is:

>> Where can we use AI?

That question naturally produces a list of tasks that might become faster, cheaper or easier.

A better strategic question is:

>> Where is AI changing your economics?

This changes the conversation. It moves AI away from being primarily a technology deployment question and places it inside the underlying mechanics of the business.

? Where does customer value change?
? Where does the cost of delivering something change materially?
? Where does work no longer need to be organised in the same way?
? Where does faster or cheaper intelligence alter a decision?
? Where does a business become possible that previously was not?

These are economic questions before they are technology questions.

## Productivity is not the same as transformation

Many of the first benefits of generative AI are productivity benefits.

| A report that once took three hours takes thirty minutes.
| A developer produces code faster.
| A customer service agent handles more cases.
| A marketing team produces more content.

These improvements can be valuable. But making an existing activity 20 percent more efficient does not necessarily change the economics of the business.

> Transformation begins when the economics move enough to require a different answer.

Consider a workflow that historically required ten people.

Reducing the effort by 15 percent is an efficiency programme.

Redesigning the workflow so that AI performs most of the coordination, analysis and execution while three people provide judgement and exception management is an operating model question.

::: diagram efficiency-vs-redesign

The technology may be similar. The economic consequence is not.

## Value can move before cost does

AI discussions frequently begin with cost reduction. Yet one of its largest effects may be on what customers value.

When intelligence becomes cheap and widely available, capabilities that once differentiated a product may become expected features. Other capabilities become newly valuable.

| Some services become easier to personalise.
| Some propositions become possible to deliver at dramatically lower marginal cost.
| Some expertise that once had to be delivered manually can become embedded in a product.

The strategic question is therefore not simply:

>> “How can AI make this product cheaper?”

It may be:

>> “Does AI change what the customer should actually be buying?”

That can alter pricing, packaging, distribution and ultimately the business model.

## Work has an economic architecture

Every organisation contains assumptions about work.

- Which tasks require people.
- How information moves.
- Where decisions are made.
- Who approves what.
- Which activities are bundled into particular roles.

Those assumptions were built under previous technological constraints. AI changes some of those constraints.

The opportunity is therefore larger than putting AI inside existing workflows. Organisations need to ask whether the workflow itself still makes sense.

When AI can analyse information, coordinate activity, produce outputs, interact with systems and increasingly execute multi-step processes, the boundary between job, workflow and software starts to move.

That has consequences for cost. But it also affects speed, capacity, organisational structure and management.

## Operating economics change too

AI transformation often becomes fragmented because experimentation happens faster than organisational decision-making.

| Different teams select different tools.
| Pilots proliferate.
| Ownership becomes unclear.
| Technology teams focus on architecture while business units focus on use cases.

Eventually the question becomes organisational:

? Who owns AI?
? What should be centralised?
? What belongs inside business units?
? How are opportunities prioritised?
? Who can stop an initiative?
? How should investment be measured?
? What risks require enterprise-level governance?

The economic value of AI depends partly on answering these questions well. A technically successful pilot that cannot move through the organisation has little enterprise value.

## AI changes the economics of building

Perhaps the least appreciated change concerns growth.

The cost of creating a new digital product, testing an idea, conducting research, producing content, supporting customers and writing software is changing. That alters the economics of venture creation.

Established businesses should therefore ask not only how AI improves the core business, but what new products, services or ventures become feasible because of it.

Some opportunities will be incremental. Others may create businesses whose economics would not have worked five years ago.

This is why AI strategy and growth strategy are beginning to converge.

| A redesigned workflow may create a new margin structure.
| A new cost structure may allow a different proposition.
| A new capability may enable a venture.
| A venture may eventually require a different operating model.

That is why a list of AI use cases is not an AI strategy.

## Start where the economics move

Every organisation will use AI. That does not mean every use matters equally.

The strategic task is to identify where AI changes the underlying economics enough to deserve disproportionate attention.

| That may be a workflow.
| A proposition.
| A decision.
| An operating model.
| Or an entirely new business.

The technology matters. But the economic consequence matters more.
