Artificial intelligence creates an unusual strategic problem. It can potentially affect almost every function in an organisation.

| Sales. Operations. Finance.
| Customer service. Technology. Human resources.
| Marketing. Strategy. Product development.

This abundance of possibilities can make AI strategy harder rather than easier.

Organisations respond by creating inventories. Use-case libraries. Maturity models. Technology roadmaps. Pilot portfolios.

These mechanisms are useful for management. They are less useful for answering the larger question:

>> What does AI actually change about this business?

The Four Fundamentals provide a simpler frame. Every business, regardless of sector, ultimately has to answer four questions:

AI can change each one.

:: The Four Fundamentals is Comet’s framework for examining where AI changes four core dimensions of a business: value, work, organisation and growth.

## 1. Value

### {{fundamentals.0.question}}

Every business exists because somebody values something enough to pay for it. AI can change both sides of that equation.

It can change what customers expect. It can lower the cost of delivering sophisticated capabilities. It can personalise products and services. It can make previously scarce expertise more accessible. It can commoditise features that once differentiated competitors. And it can enable entirely different revenue models.

Leadership teams should ask:

? Where is customer value moving?
? What becomes easier for competitors to replicate?
? What becomes more valuable?
? Where might willingness to pay change?
? Can intelligence become part of the product?
? Can a service become a product?
? Can a product become a continuously delivered service?
? Where does AI materially change margin?

The important question is not simply whether AI improves today's proposition. It is whether [the proposition itself should change](/value-creation).

## 2. Work

### {{fundamentals.1.question}}

Most organisations are structured around assumptions about human labour.

| Someone reads the information.
| Someone interprets it.
| Someone passes it to somebody else.
| Someone prepares an output.
| Someone checks it.
| Someone enters the result into another system.

Software has historically automated individual steps. AI increasingly affects the connective tissue between them.

This means the unit of analysis should move from task automation towards [workflow redesign](/work-redesign).

Leadership teams should ask:

? Which activities genuinely require human judgement?
? Which can become automated?
? Where can AI coordinate several steps rather than assisting one?
? Which approvals exist because information was historically difficult to evaluate?
? How should roles change when AI performs more analysis and execution?
? Which skills become more important?
? How should performance be measured?

The objective is not a workplace without people. It is an organisation where people spend their attention where human judgement creates the greatest value.

## 3. Organisation

### {{fundamentals.2.question}}

Technology does not transform an enterprise by itself.

Organisations transform when they change how decisions are made, resources are allocated, capabilities are built and accountability is distributed.

AI therefore creates [operating model questions](/operating-model).

? Who sets enterprise priorities?
? Who owns the underlying platforms?
? Who approves high-risk applications?
? What should be centralised?
? What should remain close to the business?
? How does an organisation avoid hundreds of disconnected pilots?
? How are AI investments assessed?
? How should governance work without slowing everything down?
? What capabilities belong inside the organisation?
? Which should come from partners?

These are not secondary implementation questions. They determine whether AI remains experimentation or becomes operating capability.

## 4. Growth

### {{fundamentals.3.question}}

The final fundamental is the most forward-looking. AI does not only improve existing organisations. It changes what can be built.

| The cost of software development is falling.
| Research can happen faster.
| Customer interaction can scale differently.
| Personalised services can operate with radically different labour economics.
| Expertise can be embedded into digital propositions.
| Small teams can build capabilities that once required substantial organisations.

This changes [venture economics](/venture-building). Leadership teams should therefore ask:

? What new markets become attractive?
? What propositions become economically feasible?
? What could we now deliver at a different cost?
? Which internal capability could become an external product?
? Where could AI create an entirely new category?
? Should we build, buy or partner?
? Which ideas deserve investment?

## The fundamentals interact

These four questions should not be treated as independent workstreams. They affect one another.

Imagine an organisation redesigns a complex workflow around AI. The immediate effect may be productivity. But the new workflow could also change the cost structure. That could make a lower-priced proposition commercially viable. The proposition could reach a new customer segment. That could become a new business. The new business might eventually require a different operating model.

This is why AI transformation cannot be reduced to a technology roadmap.

## A leadership framework, not a maturity model

The Four Fundamentals are deliberately not an AI maturity model.

Maturity models typically ask:

>> How advanced are we?

The Four Fundamentals ask:

>> Where does change matter?

An organisation can be technologically sophisticated and still invest in the wrong opportunities. Another can have limited AI maturity but possess a single opportunity capable of materially changing its economics.

Leadership therefore needs both capability and prioritisation.

> The first tells you what you can do. The second tells you what is worth doing.

## A simpler AI conversation

AI will become embedded in most organisations.

The strategic advantage will not come from simply having access to the technology. It will come from making better choices about where to apply it and being willing to redesign the business when the economics justify it.
